You post a Reel that ends with “Comment GUIDE and I’ll send it to you.” By the evening, 600 people are waiting for a link.

Somewhere in your requests, a woman asks whether your 12-week programme works with a shoulder injury. She’s ready to pay. She’s also message 214.

Then the ads find you, promising an AI that answers DMs in your voice while you sleep.

There are four kinds of AI and automation tools for Instagram DMs. Keyword and comment-to-DM tools such as ManyChat send a message you wrote whenever someone uses a trigger word. AI agents write and send replies on their own. Approve-first tools draft replies for you to check and send. Or you can paste messages into a general chatbot such as ChatGPT. Any tool you connect to your account should use Meta’s official Instagram API and never ask for your password.

The choice comes down to who presses send: automation for predictable, high-volume messages, and you for anything where a wrong answer could cost a sale or someone’s trust.

## 1\. Keyword and comment-to-DM automation, such as ManyChat

You write the messages in advance and pick a trigger, such as a keyword in a DM or a comment on a particular post. When someone uses it, the tool sends your message, usually with a link. ManyChat works this way (it also offers AI features that write replies), and [Hootsuite’s inbox](https://www.hootsuite.com/platform/social-media-inbox) offers keyword-triggered comment replies and DMs for Instagram posts and Reels.

On Meta’s official API, a DM sent because someone commented is a [private reply](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/private-replies). Meta allows one message to the commenter, sent within seven days of the comment, and more only if they reply (then within 24 hours of their reply). It also caps private replies to post and Reel comments at [750 an hour per account](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/overview/), so if 3,000 people comment in the first hour, some will wait for their link.

This suits giving the same thing to lots of people, like a free guide or a waitlist link. Nobody needs a personal note with a PDF.

It goes wrong when a message doesn’t fit the script. If your trigger word is GUIDE and someone writes “the guide link isn’t opening”, they may get the same link again, and a buyer’s real question can get the giveaway reply. Aggressive set-ups also brush against Meta’s spam rules (more below).

## 2\. AI agents that reply to DMs on their own

An AI agent reads each message and writes the reply itself, drawing on material you give it, like your FAQs and prices. It sends without waiting for you, though the agents below let you decide when a person takes over.

In June 2026 Meta announced its own, [Meta Business Agent](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/06/meta-business-agent/), which can answer questions, book appointments and qualify leads, and said it was expanding the agent to Instagram. Instagram’s [AI auto-replies](https://help.instagram.com/317190238019695), where a creator AI you built in Meta AI Studio answers messages for you, are being introduced gradually and may not be available to you yet. [Meta also said in August 2026](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/07/create-your-own-custom-ai-with-ai-studio/) that people can no longer create new AI characters, or edit existing ones, in AI Studio.

Agents suit accounts with hundreds of questions a day that have one right answer, like when the replay goes up or whether you ship to Dubai. If a wrong answer is cheap, an agent buys you time.

The risk is that it improvises, and in a sales chat a wrong answer becomes a promise. Picture a fitness coach’s agent (example) telling a follower who is four months postpartum that the programme is “suitable for everyone”, because the sales page says all levels are welcome. [Respond.io’s help centre](https://respond.io/help/ai-agents/ai-agents-known-limitations-and-workarounds) lists known limits, with workarounds where it has them: its agents reply on every turn by default, can’t see the image or video in a story reply, and have nothing to read when someone replies to a story without typing.

## 3\. Approve-first tools: AI drafts, you send

An approve-first tool reads the conversation, writes a draft and stops there. Nothing goes out until you’ve checked it and pressed send. Some team inboxes have a feature like this: Hootsuite’s offers AI “smart replies” that suggest responses to comments and messages.

DealDock, which publishes this guide, is building this kind of tool for creators. Its small, application-only pilot is built to draft each reply in the creator’s own texting style, which they can edit, and to send nothing without their approval, follow-ups included. The pilot connects through Meta’s official Instagram API and never asks for a password.

This suits conversations where the reply affects money or trust, like a coaching enquiry or a brand asking for your rates. The draft saves typing, and you add what only you know, such as whether there’s still a seat in the March batch.

You trade speed for control. Someone who messages at 2am waits until you open the app, and drafts nobody checks just sit there. It’s also easy to approve without reading, so read each one: it goes out under your name.

## 4\. Pasting DMs into ChatGPT or another chatbot

Copy a message into ChatGPT or Gemini, ask for a reply in your tone, then paste it back into Instagram. It’s free to start, and because nothing connects to your account, you send every word yourself. It suits a few tricky messages a week, like a brand asking for “exclusive rights” with no end date.

It’s slow, though, and the chatbot only knows what you paste in. You’re also handing someone else’s message to another company, so strip out names, numbers and anything sensitive first. Check how the chatbot handles your data too: Google’s [Gemini Apps Privacy Hub](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en), for example, says some chats are read by human reviewers and asks people not to enter confidential information they wouldn’t want a reviewer to see.

## Auto-replies vs human-approved drafts: how the four compare

![Four reply methods compared: keyword automation, AI agents, human-approved drafts, and copy-and-paste chatbots](/blog/ai-instagram-dm-tools/reply-methods.webp)

| Type | Who sends the reply | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keyword automation | The tool, in seconds, using text you wrote | Giveaways and link requests |
| AI agent | The tool, in seconds, in words it chooses | Lots of repeat questions with one right answer |
| Approve-first drafts | You, after reading the draft | Sales, coaching and brand enquiries |
| Copy-paste chatbot | You, after pasting it back | A few hard replies a week |

You can mix them: automation sends the freebie, and you answer the follow-up questions.

## Is ManyChat safe? What makes any DM tool safe for your account

Meta’s business site features ManyChat as a [Meta Business Partner](https://www.facebook.com/business/marketing-partners/case-studies/manychat_bb-littles) in a business messaging case study, and using a tool built on Meta’s official API isn’t against Instagram’s rules in itself. What matters is how a tool connects and how you use it.

An official tool sends you to [Instagram’s or Facebook’s own login window](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/overview/) to approve specific permissions, such as managing messages. The tool gets an access token, not your password, and it only works with professional (business or creator) accounts. Depending on how it connects, it may also ask you to switch on Allow access to messages, under Connected tools in Instagram’s message settings.

A tool that asks for your password, or runs a browser that logs in and clicks around as you, works outside that system. Instagram’s help page on [accounts restricted for data scraping](https://help.instagram.com/740480200552298) lists “You may have provided your username and password to a third-party app or website” as a possible reason.

Then there’s how you use it. Meta’s [spam rules](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/spam/), which apply to Instagram, ban posting, sharing or engaging with content at very high frequencies, whether manually or automatically. Accounts that post repetitive content can be restricted even at lower volumes. Answering people who asked for something is different from sending the same message to everyone.

## Meta’s 24-hour rule and the 7-day Human Agent tag

Under Meta’s [messaging policy](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/messenger-platform/policy/policy-overview) for apps on its API, a business has a “standard messaging window” to reply, which for most messages lasts 24 hours after the person writes. Inside it, a tool can reply automatically. After it, the only message tag Instagram supports is Human Agent, a separate seven-day window that Meta describes as letting businesses “manually respond”. Apps need Meta’s approval to use it, through App Review and business verification, and Meta warns that using tags outside their approved uses may restrict your ability to send messages.

So a tool can send the giveaway link and answer questions the same day, but it can’t send an automated “just checking in!” a week later. That follow-up is yours to send from the Instagram app, where these windows don’t apply. Here’s [how to follow up on a coaching enquiry](/blog/follow-up-coaching-enquiry) without sounding pushy.

## Do you have to tell people they’re talking to a bot?

Sometimes the law requires it, and it’s good practice everywhere. Meta’s messaging policy says that where the law requires it, automated chats must say so at the start of a conversation, after a significant lapse of time and when a chat moves from a person to automation. Meta recommends it even where no law applies, and when it launched creator AIs in 2024, it said their replies would be [clearly labelled](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/07/create-your-own-custom-ai-with-ai-studio/) as AI.

California’s bot disclosure law is one example. [SB 1001 (Business and Professions Code section 17940 onwards)](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_17941), in force since July 2019, makes it unlawful to use a bot online with the intent to mislead someone in California about its artificial identity, knowingly deceiving them to encourage a purchase or sale (or influence a vote). Disclosing clearly and conspicuously that it’s a bot means you aren’t liable under that section.

The law defines a bot as an automated online account where all or substantially all of its actions or posts aren’t the result of a person, so whether a mostly human account running an AI agent counts is a question for a lawyer, ideally one who knows where you and your buyers are. ## A checklist for choosing an Instagram DM tool

1.  Does it use Meta’s official Instagram API? You should log in on Instagram’s or Facebook’s own screen and approve permissions. If the company can’t say how it connects, skip it.
2.  Does it ask for your password? Then stop. A tool on the official API never needs it.
3.  Can you approve every reply before it’s sent? Check follow-ups and comment replies separately.
4.  Where are your messages stored, and for how long? Ask whether DMs are used to train AI models and what happens to them when you leave.
5.  Can you export and delete your data? Removing an app in Instagram’s settings (Website permissions, then Apps and websites) limits it to your public information, but the app may still hold what it stored before, so ask the tool how to delete it.
6.  What happens when the AI isn’t sure? It should hold the message for you or hand it to a person, not guess.
7.  What will it cost at your volume? Ask what the price is based on, such as contacts or AI replies, and work it out for your biggest launch month.

## Do you need a tool at all?

If you can read every DM yourself within a day, probably not. Instagram’s folders, saved replies and a simple [enquiry tracker](/blog/dm-enquiry-tracker-template) will cover it, and our guide to [managing Instagram DMs as a coach](/blog/manage-instagram-dms-coach) shows the routine. Add comment-to-DM automation when you’re sending the same link hundreds of times, and try approve-first drafts when typing replies to real buyers is eating your evenings.

Whatever you try, send it a test DM from a friend’s account first, and read exactly what comes back.
